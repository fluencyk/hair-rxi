## 1. Domain Scope and Positioning

### 1.1 Working Definition

Business Intelligence and Analytics (BI&A) concerns the systematic use of business data, analytical methods, information systems, and domain knowledge to understand organizational performance, investigate business problems, support decisions, and evaluate outcomes.

Business Intelligence (BI) commonly emphasizes the integration, organization, monitoring, reporting, and visualization of business information. Business Analytics (BA) extends this capability through diagnostic investigation, statistical analysis, forecasting, predictive modeling, and decision-oriented methods.

The two terms overlap, and their exact scope varies across institutions and industries. For this curriculum model, BI&A is treated as an integrated domain spanning business problem framing, data management, analysis, communication, and decision support.

A successful BI&A activity does not end when a dashboard or model is produced. Its purpose is to help stakeholders make better-informed decisions and assess whether those decisions generate the intended value.

### 1.2 Primary Scope

The candidate cognition space includes:

- Business processes, organizational context, and decision problems.
- Business metrics, key performance indicators, and measurement.
- Data literacy, data quality, and data governance.
- Data collection, preparation, integration, and modeling.
- Database concepts, SQL, and analytical data structures.
- Descriptive and diagnostic analytics.
- Statistical reasoning and exploratory data analysis.
- Data visualization, dashboards, and business reporting.
- Predictive analytics and introductory machine learning.
- Forecasting, optimization, simulation, and decision support.
- Business experimentation and causal reasoning.
- Analytical storytelling and stakeholder communication.
- Analytics deployment, monitoring, and value realization.
- Responsible analytics, privacy, fairness, and organizational governance.

### 1.3 Boundary with Adjacent Domains

| Adjacent Domain | Relationship | Boundary Principle |
|---|---|---|
| Modern Software Development | Data pipelines, application integration, software delivery | BI&A uses software and data systems but does not replace general software engineering |
| Applied AI and Intelligent Systems | Predictive models, machine learning, AI-assisted analysis | BI&A focuses on business questions and analytical value; advanced AI methods remain shared or adjacent capabilities |
| Systems Engineering | System performance, requirements, feedback, decision analysis | BI&A contributes evidence and measurement; systems engineering addresses broader system integration and lifecycle concerns |
| Industrial Design | Product research, customer evidence, product performance | BI&A provides analytical support without absorbing the complete design process |
| HCI / UX / UI | User research, interaction analytics, usability metrics | BI&A emphasizes analytical interpretation and decisions; HCI emphasizes human-computer interaction and experience |
| Foundation Core | Quantitative reasoning, research literacy, communication, basic computing | Shared foundations should be reused rather than duplicated unnecessarily |
| Advanced Core | Modeling, optimization, complex systems, verification, decision support | Advanced methods may support BI&A projects without becoming exclusively BI&A topics |

### 1.4 Important Distinctions

- BI&A is not merely dashboard creation or spreadsheet reporting.
- Data analytics is not automatically business analytics; the business context and decision relevance matter.
- Correlation does not establish causation.
- A metric is not necessarily a meaningful measure of organizational success.
- A predictive model estimates outcomes; it does not by itself determine the best action.
- A recommendation is not automatically a good decision without constraints, trade-offs, and stakeholder context.
- A technically correct analysis can still be misleading if the data, assumptions, or question are inappropriate.
- A polished visualization does not guarantee analytical validity.
- The inventory is a candidate map, not a claim that every listed topic belongs in every course.

## 5. D4 — Processes and Methods

### 5.1 Business Question to Analytical Plan

- Identify the business problem or opportunity.
- Clarify stakeholders and decision ownership.
- Define the decision to be supported.
- Specify business objectives and constraints.
- Define measurable success criteria.
- Determine whether analytics is appropriate.
- Translate the business problem into an analytical question.
- Select an appropriate analytical approach.
- Establish scope, assumptions, risks, and baseline measures.
- Obtain stakeholder agreement before substantial implementation.

### 5.2 Data Discovery and Preparation

- Identify relevant data sources.
- Confirm access, permissions, and usage constraints.
- Assess data availability and quality.
- Define data requirements and granularity.
- Extract and integrate relevant data.
- Clean and transform data.
- Validate definitions and transformations.
- Document lineage and assumptions.
- Establish a repeatable preparation workflow.
- Revisit the analytical question when data limitations change the feasible scope.

### 5.3 Exploratory Analysis and Insight Development

- Inspect data structure and quality.
- Summarize distributions and relationships.
- Identify trends, anomalies, and segments.
- Formulate candidate explanations.
- Distinguish observation from interpretation.
- Investigate alternative explanations.
- Test important assumptions where appropriate.
- Prioritize findings by relevance and reliability.
- Translate findings into business implications.

### 5.4 Statistical and Predictive Modeling

- Select a method aligned with the question.
- Establish a baseline and evaluation plan.
- Prepare data without leakage.
- Train or estimate the model.
- Evaluate assumptions and performance.
- Compare candidate approaches.
- Analyze errors and limitations.
- Assess robustness and relevant subgroups.
- Communicate uncertainty and practical significance.
- Decide whether the model provides sufficient value for the intended use.

### 5.5 Dashboard and Reporting Development

- Identify intended users and decisions.
- Define metrics and business rules.
- Establish consistent data definitions.
- Select relevant visualizations.
- Organize the information hierarchy.
- Develop reports or dashboards.
- Validate calculations and filters.
- Test interpretation and usability.
- Document data refresh, limitations, and ownership.
- Monitor whether the reporting product supports actual decisions.

### 5.6 Decision Support and Recommendations

- Translate analytical findings into alternatives.
- Identify objectives and constraints.
- Estimate expected benefits and costs.
- Consider uncertainty and downside risk.
- Compare feasible options.
- Evaluate trade-offs and sensitivities.
- Incorporate relevant expert judgment.
- Communicate a recommendation and its rationale.
- Identify implementation requirements.
- Define how the decision's outcomes will be evaluated.

### 5.7 Deployment and Operational Monitoring

- Determine how analytical outputs will reach users or systems.
- Establish data and model refresh procedures.
- Define ownership and operational responsibilities.
- Monitor data quality and analytical performance.
- Track changes in business conditions.
- Identify drift and changing assumptions.
- Record failures and corrective actions.
- Reassess whether the solution still supports its intended decision.
- Retire or revise solutions when value or validity deteriorates.

### 5.8 Governance and Responsible Practice

- Confirm legitimate data use and permissions.
- Apply appropriate privacy and security controls.
- Document assumptions and transformations.
- Make analytical limitations visible.
- Review potential bias and harm.
- Preserve reproducibility where feasible.
- Establish human oversight for consequential decisions.
- Maintain decision and model records.
- Ensure that claims are supported by available evidence.

## 11. Coverage Audit and Selection Readiness

This section supports review and refinement. It does not certify that the inventory is complete.

### 11.1 Coverage Questions

Before using the inventory to construct a curriculum, examine whether it adequately represents:

- Business context and problem framing.
- Metrics, KPIs, and performance measurement.
- Data literacy, data quality, and data governance.
- Data preparation, querying, and modeling.
- Descriptive and diagnostic analysis.
- Statistical reasoning and uncertainty.
- Visualization, dashboards, and communication.
- Predictive modeling and evaluation.
- Decision analysis, forecasting, and optimization.
- Deployment, monitoring, and value realization.
- Responsible use, privacy, and bias.
- Cross-disciplinary application and collaboration.

### 11.2 Common Modeling Risks

**Risk 1 — Reducing BI&A to Dashboards**

Dashboards are important artifacts, but do not replace problem framing, data quality, analytical reasoning, or decision evaluation.

**Risk 2 — Treating Data Availability as Business Value**

Having data does not prove that a useful question has been identified or that the available data can answer it.

**Risk 3 — Confusing Correlation with Causation**

Observed associations may reflect confounding, selection bias, reverse causality, or other mechanisms.

**Risk 4 — Treating Predictive Accuracy as Decision Quality**

A model may perform well statistically while being irrelevant, costly, unfair, or operationally unusable.

**Risk 5 — Overloading the Domain with Computer Science**

Database and programming literacy are important, but general software engineering and advanced infrastructure should not be duplicated unnecessarily.

**Risk 6 — Overloading the Domain with Statistics or AI**

BI&A uses statistical and AI methods selectively. It does not need to contain the full depth of statistics, machine learning, or AI research.

**Risk 7 — Confusing Metrics with Objectives**

Metrics can shape behavior and create unintended incentives. Metric selection requires contextual and organizational judgment.

**Risk 8 — Omitting Deployment and Follow-Up**

An analysis that is never used, monitored, or evaluated may not produce sustained value.

**Risk 9 — Mixing Abstraction Levels**

Business concepts, analytical methods, software tools, deliverables, and competencies should remain distinguishable even when closely related.

### 11.3 Inventory Versus Curriculum

The inventory answers:

- What knowledge, methods, tools, artifacts, and competencies might matter in BI&A?
- What relationships exist among them and adjacent domains?
- What candidate prerequisites and capability levels may be relevant?

The curriculum-construction process must separately answer:

- Which outcomes are appropriate for the target learners?
- Which business contexts are accessible and meaningful?
- Which analytical methods are necessary for those outcomes?
- What depth is feasible within the available teaching time?
- Which data, software, and infrastructure are available?
- What artifacts demonstrate learning?
- How will analytical validity and decision usefulness be evaluated?
- What privacy, security, and ethics constraints apply?

### 11.4 Selection Readiness Checklist

- [ ] Domain scope and boundaries are sufficiently clear.
- [ ] Major business, data, and analytical areas have been inventoried.
- [ ] The eight observation dimensions have been considered.
- [ ] Candidate omissions and duplications have been reviewed.

- [ ] Tools are distinguished from transferable competencies.
- [ ] Analytical artifacts are distinguished from learning outcomes.
