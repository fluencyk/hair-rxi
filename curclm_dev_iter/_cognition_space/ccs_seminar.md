## 1. Purpose and Scope

This document establishes an initial candidate cognition-space inventory for **Seminar**.

Its purpose is to identify the potential knowledge domains, concepts, research methods, inquiry processes, tools, artifacts, and competencies that may contribute to a seminar-oriented learning experience.

This inventory is a discovery and modeling resource, not a finalized syllabus. The inclusion of a topic does not imply that every student must study, practice, or demonstrate every item.

The inventory should support subsequent decisions about:

- The educational purpose and identity of Seminar
- Research and inquiry expectations
- Critical reading and evidence-based reasoning
- Academic discussion and intellectual exchange
- Research communication and presentation
- Written, oral, visual, or demonstrative outcomes
- Learner prerequisites and levels of independence
- Assessment criteria and observable evidence
- Feasible course structures and instructional support

The governing principle is to identify the candidate cognition space before narrowing it according to learner needs, intended outcomes, course duration, available resources, and the broader curriculum architecture.

---

## 2. Working Course Definition

**Seminar** develops students' capacity to investigate questions, engage critically with existing knowledge, construct evidence-based interpretations or arguments, exchange ideas, and communicate their findings to an audience.

Depending on learner readiness and the course's final positioning, students may engage in guided inquiry, literature and case-study analysis, research proposal development, small-scale investigation, design or engineering project reflection, academic discussion, and structured presentation.

The course emphasizes the relationship between:

- **Question:** What is worth investigating, and why?
- **Knowledge:** What is already known, claimed, or debated?
- **Evidence:** What information supports or challenges a claim?
- **Reasoning:** How should evidence be interpreted and connected to a conclusion?
- **Communication:** How can ideas and findings be presented clearly and responsibly?
- **Reflection:** What remains uncertain, limited, or worth investigating next?

Seminar is not necessarily a conventional research-methods course, a lecture series, or a requirement to produce a formal academic paper in every instance.

Its final form may combine inquiry, discussion, critical reading, research practice, and scholarly communication in proportions appropriate to the intended learners.

## 3. Candidate Scope and Boundaries

### 3.1 Core Focus

The candidate scope includes:

1. Identifying and refining questions, problems, or topics for inquiry.
2. Locating and evaluating relevant sources.
3. Understanding claims, arguments, evidence, and assumptions.
4. Comparing perspectives and identifying disagreements.
5. Formulating researchable questions or defensible positions.
6. Selecting appropriate methods of inquiry.
7. Organizing, analyzing, and interpreting evidence.
8. Constructing clear and logically supported arguments.
9. Participating in structured academic discussion.
10. Presenting findings and responding to questions.
11. Documenting sources and acknowledging intellectual contributions.
12. Reflecting on limitations, uncertainty, and future inquiry.

### 3.2 Candidate Seminar Modes

The term *Seminar* may represent several related but distinct educational modes.

#### A. Discussion-Oriented Seminar

Emphasis:

- Critical reading
- Question generation
- Discussion preparation
- Evidence-based dialogue
- Comparing perspectives
- Responding to and building on others' ideas

#### B. Research-Oriented Seminar

Emphasis:

- Research-question development
- Literature and source review
- Method selection
- Evidence collection or analysis
- Interpretation and argumentation
- Research communication

#### C. Project-Reflection Seminar

Emphasis:

- Examining decisions made during a design or engineering project
- Connecting practice with relevant concepts and prior work
- Analyzing failures, trade-offs, and unexpected results
- Evaluating evidence from project development
- Communicating transferable insights

#### D. Presentation-Oriented Seminar

Emphasis:

- Organizing an argument or research narrative
- Presenting evidence and findings
- Using visual and verbal communication effectively
- Responding to questions
- Participating in peer critique

These modes may be combined, but the final course should have an identifiable primary purpose rather than attempting to maximize all four simultaneously.

### 3.3 Relationship to Other Courses

| Related course | Primary emphasis | Distinction in Seminar |
|---|---|---|
| Fundamentals of Engineering | Engineering foundations, scientific reasoning, and introductory problem-solving | May provide the subject matter for inquiry, but Seminar focuses on examining and communicating questions and evidence |
| Systems Design and Abstract Modeling | System boundaries, abstraction, modeling, architecture, and system-level reasoning | May discuss or critique models, but Seminar emphasizes inquiry, argumentation, and intellectual exchange |
| Applied Convergence of Design and Technology | Integrating design and technology to develop and evaluate an outcome | May examine the project's rationale and findings, but Seminar focuses on critical interpretation and communication rather than implementation alone |
| HCI / UX / UI | Human-computer interaction, interface design, and user experience | May provide research topics and methods without requiring every seminar to be an HCI investigation |
| Modern Software Development | Software engineering and development practice | May supply technical cases or project evidence without making software implementation the course's universal objective |
| Applied AI and Intelligent Systems | AI capabilities, intelligent systems, and their applications | May supply research questions and case studies without requiring AI-specific content |
| Robotics | Robotic systems, physical computing, sensing, actuation, and control | May provide a domain for investigation without making robotics a prerequisite |
| Industrial Design and Technologies | Product design, materials, manufacturing, and product development | May provide cases or projects for analysis without turning Seminar into a product-design studio |
| Business Intelligence and Analytics | Data analysis, interpretation, and decision support | May contribute evidence and analytical methods where relevant, while Seminar retains its broader inquiry and communication focus |

These distinctions are provisional and should be reviewed against the full curriculum architecture.

### 3.4 What Seminar Is Not

Seminar should not automatically become:

- A sequence of instructor lectures with little student inquiry.
- A public-speaking course without substantive intellectual content.
- A literature-summary course that rewards collecting sources rather than evaluating them.
- A research-methods course that attempts to teach every possible method.
- A paper-writing course in which formal structure substitutes for sound reasoning.
- A project showcase without critical reflection or evidence.
- A requirement to produce publication-level research regardless of learner readiness.
- A generic discussion class without clear preparation, participation, or learning expectations.

Candidate knowledge domains that may contribute to Seminar.

### A. Academic and Intellectual Inquiry

- The nature and purpose of academic inquiry
- Differences between questions, claims, hypotheses, and conclusions
- Relationship between existing knowledge and new investigation
- Disciplinary perspectives and conventions
- Interdisciplinary inquiry
- Intellectual disagreement and competing explanations
- The role of uncertainty in knowledge development
- Distinction between description, interpretation, evaluation, and explanation

- Citation practices and attribution

### C. Research and Evidence

- Research questions and objectives
- Literature and precedent review
- Qualitative and quantitative evidence
- Observational, experimental, documentary, and computational evidence
- Research design at an introductory level
- Data interpretation and limitations
- Evidence quality and uncertainty
- Validity and reliability where appropriate to the method
- Alternative explanations and contradictory findings

### D. Argumentation and Critical Interpretation

- Claims, reasons, and supporting evidence
- Logical structure of arguments
- Assumptions and implicit premises
- Counterarguments and alternative interpretations
- Distinguishing correlation from causation where relevant
- Strength of evidence
- Scope and limits of conclusions
- Synthesis across multiple sources
- Intellectual humility and willingness to revise a position

### E. Academic Communication

- Academic and professional writing
- Oral presentation
- Visual communication of ideas and evidence
- Research posters and short-form research summaries
- Structured discussion and debate
- Question formulation and response
- Peer feedback and critique
- Audience awareness
- Responsible scholarly communication

### F. Research Ethics and Responsibility

- Academic integrity
- Attribution and citation
- Plagiarism and inappropriate reuse
- Responsible data handling
- Research participation and consent where relevant
- Privacy and confidentiality
- Representation of evidence without distortion
- Responsible use and disclosure of AI-assisted work
- Research limitations and conflicts of interest

**Modeling note:** These domains are candidate knowledge resources. Their inclusion should depend on the seminar's primary mode and intended learning outcomes.

## 4.4 Processes and Methods

Candidate workflows for moving from an initial interest to an evidence-supported and communicated outcome.

### A. Inquiry Development Workflow

1. Identify a topic, problem, or phenomenon.
2. Conduct preliminary exploration.
3. Identify what is known and what remains unclear.
4. Formulate a bounded question or inquiry objective.
5. Assess relevance, feasibility, and available evidence.
6. Define the intended output and evaluation criteria.

### B. Source Investigation Workflow

1. Translate the question into search terms.
2. Locate potentially relevant sources.
3. Screen sources for relevance and credibility.
4. Read selected sources critically.
5. Extract claims, methods, evidence, and limitations.
6. Compare perspectives and findings.
7. Organize sources by theme, argument, or disagreement.
8. Identify unresolved questions and evidence gaps.

### C. Evidence-Based Reasoning Workflow

1. Formulate a provisional claim or interpretation.
2. Identify the evidence needed to support or challenge it.
3. Gather or select relevant evidence.
4. Evaluate the quality and limitations of that evidence.
5. Consider alternative interpretations.
6. Refine the claim and its scope.
7. Document the reasoning connecting evidence to conclusions.

### D. Small-Scale Research Workflow

1. Define the research question.
2. Select an appropriate and feasible method.
3. Identify ethical and practical constraints.
4. Prepare a research or investigation plan.
5. Collect or obtain relevant evidence.
6. Organize and analyze the evidence.
7. Interpret findings in relation to the question.
8. State limitations and unresolved uncertainties.

This workflow is optional and should be used only when conducting a small-scale investigation is part of the seminar's intended purpose.

### E. Discussion Workflow

1. Prepare through reading, research, or written reflection.
2. Identify key claims, questions, and uncertainties.
3. Present an interpretation supported by evidence.
4. Listen to and clarify other perspectives.
5. Ask questions that advance understanding.
6. Examine disagreements and counterarguments.
7. Revise or qualify a position where justified.
8. Record useful conclusions and remaining questions.

### F. Research Communication Workflow

1. Identify the audience and communication purpose.
2. Select the principal claim, finding, or insight.
3. Organize the reasoning and supporting evidence.
4. Choose an appropriate written, oral, or visual format.
5. Prepare supporting visuals or documentation.
6. Present the argument or findings.
7. Respond to questions and critique.
8. Revise the output where appropriate.
9. Reflect on limitations and future inquiry.

### G. Reflection Workflow

1. Compare initial expectations with actual findings.
2. Identify assumptions that were supported or challenged.
3. Examine methodological and evidential limitations.
4. Identify changes in understanding.
5. Record unresolved questions.
6. Formulate plausible next steps.

**Modeling note:** These workflows are not necessarily separate modules. They may be integrated into a single inquiry cycle or distributed across multiple seminar sessions.

## 4.7 Competencies and Performance

Candidate capabilities students may develop and demonstrate.

### A. Inquiry and Question Formulation

- Identify meaningful questions.
- Distinguish a broad topic from an answerable question.
- Define an appropriate scope.
- Explain why a question matters.
- Assess basic feasibility and evidence availability.
- Refine questions as understanding develops.

### B. Critical Reading and Source Evaluation

- Identify the central claim of a source.
- Distinguish claims, methods, evidence, and conclusions.
- Evaluate relevance and credibility.
- Recognize limitations and unsupported assertions.
- Compare sources with different perspectives.
- Synthesize information across multiple sources.
- Attribute ideas and evidence appropriately.

### C. Evidence-Based Reasoning

- Construct claims supported by relevant evidence.
- Explain the relationship between evidence and conclusions.
- Recognize assumptions and alternative interpretations.
- Distinguish observation from inference.
- Qualify conclusions when evidence is incomplete.
- Respond to counterarguments.
- Revise an argument in response to stronger evidence.

### D. Research Practice

- Select a feasible method of inquiry.
- Organize research tasks and sources.
- Collect or obtain relevant evidence responsibly.
- Apply appropriate introductory analysis.
- Document methods and decisions.
- Recognize ethical and practical constraints.
- Explain limitations and unresolved uncertainties.

### E. Academic Discussion

- Prepare substantively for discussion.
- Ask clear and productive questions.
- Present a reasoned interpretation.
- Listen and respond to other perspectives.
- Challenge ideas constructively.
- Build on others' contributions.
- Distinguish disagreement from personal criticism.
- Revise or qualify a position when justified.

### F. Research Communication

- Organize ideas into a coherent argument.
- Explain methods and findings clearly.
- Select appropriate visual and verbal representations.
- Communicate to an identified audience.
- Respond to questions about evidence and reasoning.
- Present limitations without overstating conclusions.
- Adapt communication to the intended format.

### G. Academic Integrity and Reflection

- Cite and attribute sources accurately.
- Distinguish original contribution from borrowed material.
- Use AI-assisted tools responsibly where permitted.
- Represent findings without intentional distortion.
- Identify uncertainty and limitations.
- Reflect on changes in understanding.
- Formulate meaningful directions for further inquiry.

**Assessment principle:** Competence should be assessed through observable evidence such as source analysis, reasoning, discussion contributions, research records, and communication outputs. Attendance or presentation polish alone is insufficient evidence of substantive learning.

## 4.8 Levels and Prerequisites

The following progression is provisional and should be adapted to the final learner profile and seminar mode.

### Level 1 — Entry

Students can:

- Identify a topic of interest.
- Ask basic questions about a text, case, or phenomenon.
- Locate and summarize a small number of supplied or guided sources.
- Distinguish a claim from a supporting example with assistance.
- Participate in structured discussion.
- Present a basic explanation of an idea.

Typical support: selected readings, guiding questions, structured notes, and explicit discussion protocols.

### Level 2 — Foundational

Students can:

- Formulate a bounded inquiry question with guidance.
- Locate relevant sources using a basic search strategy.
- Identify central claims, evidence, and limitations.
- Compare more than one perspective.
- Construct a simple evidence-supported argument.
- Participate constructively in discussion.
- Deliver a structured presentation with appropriate attribution.

Typical support: templates, source-evaluation criteria, feedback, and milestone reviews.

### Level 3 — Developing

Students can:

- Refine a research question with increasing independence.
- Synthesize multiple sources around themes or disagreements.
- Select a feasible method for a bounded investigation.
- Analyze evidence and qualify conclusions.
- Address counterarguments.
- Respond to peer critique and revise their reasoning.
- Communicate findings through a coherent written or oral output.

Typical support: periodic guidance, with students taking responsibility for most inquiry and communication decisions.

### Level 4 — Extension

Students can:

- Investigate a more ambiguous or interdisciplinary question.
- Independently identify important evidence gaps.
- Compare methodological approaches and their limitations.
- Integrate multiple forms of evidence where appropriate.
- Develop a sustained and qualified argument.
- Critically evaluate alternative interpretations.
- Defend decisions and conclusions under questioning.
- Formulate credible next steps for further investigation.

Typical support: greater independence, with advanced methods introduced only where relevant.

### Candidate Prerequisites

Potential prerequisites include:

- Basic reading comprehension
- Basic written and oral communication
- Ability to locate and record information
- Willingness to question assumptions and consider alternative perspectives
- Basic digital literacy
- Ability to follow source-attribution expectations

Formal research experience, advanced statistics, specialist disciplinary knowledge, and prior publication experience should not be assumed unless required by the final course design.

### Progression Principle

Progression should primarily reflect:

- Question quality and scope
- Independence in source discovery and evaluation
- Depth of synthesis
- Strength of evidence-based reasoning
- Appropriate use of methods
- Quality of discussion and response to critique
- Clarity and integrity of communication
- Ability to recognize limitations and uncertainty

It should not be defined solely by paper length, number of citations, presentation duration, or apparent academic sophistication.

## 5. Cross-Dimensional Relationships

The eight dimensions become educationally meaningful when connected through a coherent inquiry and communication process.

| Learning relationship | Relevant dimensions | Observable evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Turning an interest into an inquiry question | Disciplinary Knowledge; Concepts and Theories; Competencies | Topic statement, research question, scope rationale |
| Finding and evaluating relevant knowledge | Research Methods; Tools and Technologies; Competencies | Search strategy, source list, critical reading notes |
| Comparing existing claims and perspectives | Disciplinary Knowledge; Concepts and Theories; Research Methods | Source comparison, literature matrix, synthesis |
| Developing an evidence-supported argument | Concepts and Theories; Processes and Methods; Competencies | Claim-evidence outline, argument map, written analysis |
| Conducting a bounded investigation | Research Methods; Processes and Methods; Tools and Technologies | Research plan, evidence records, analysis |
| Participating in intellectual exchange | Concepts and Theories; Processes and Methods; Competencies | Discussion preparation, contributions, peer feedback |
| Communicating findings | Processes and Methods; Artifacts and Deliverables; Competencies | Report, abstract, poster, presentation |
| Reflecting on uncertainty and future inquiry | Concepts and Theories; Artifacts and Deliverables; Competencies | Limitations analysis, reflection, follow-up questions |

### Candidate End-to-End Inquiry Loop

**Interest → Question → Sources → Evidence → Interpretation → Argument → Discussion → Communication → Reflection**

This loop is provisional rather than strictly linear. Students may revisit their questions, seek additional evidence, revise interpretations, or change the scope of their inquiry.

The critical requirement is that communication should emerge from substantive inquiry and reasoning, rather than substitute for them.

## 6. Candidate Learning Activity Patterns

These patterns may help translate the cognition-space inventory into learning modules or seminar sessions.

### Pattern A — Question the Question

Students examine an initial topic and develop more focused, investigable questions.

Potential evidence:

- Initial topic statement
- Alternative questions
- Scope comparison
- Final question and rationale

### Pattern B — Read Beyond the Summary

Students examine a source's claims, evidence, methods, and limitations rather than relying exclusively on its abstract or summary.

Potential evidence:

- Critical reading notes
- Claim-evidence identification
- Method and limitation analysis
- Questions for further investigation

### Pattern C — Two Sources, Two Perspectives

Students compare sources that differ in explanation, method, evidence, or conclusion.

Potential evidence:

- Comparison matrix
- Points of agreement and disagreement
- Evidence-quality assessment
- Synthesis of unresolved issues

### Pattern D — Build an Argument from Evidence

Students develop a claim and map the evidence and reasoning needed to support it.

Potential evidence:

- Claim-evidence outline
- Argument map
- Counterargument
- Revised conclusion

### Pattern E — Research Design Clinic

Students examine a question and compare possible methods of investigation.

Potential evidence:

- Candidate methods
- Feasibility assessment
- Method-selection rationale
- Limitations and ethical considerations

### Pattern F — Structured Academic Discussion

Students prepare a position or interpretation, exchange arguments, and respond to evidence and alternative perspectives.

Potential evidence:

- Preparation notes
- Discussion contributions
- Questions and responses
- Reflection on changed or qualified views

### Pattern G — Research in Progress

Students present an unfinished investigation and receive feedback on the question, evidence, method, or argument.

Potential evidence:

- Research brief
- Preliminary findings
- Peer feedback
- Revised inquiry plan

### Pattern H — Communicate for an Audience

Students transform an inquiry into an appropriate written, oral, or visual output.

Potential evidence:

- Abstract or summary
- Presentation or poster
- Evidence visualization
- Audience-oriented explanation
- Response to questions

### Pattern I — Limitations and Next Questions

Students identify what their inquiry cannot yet establish and formulate credible next steps.

Potential evidence:

- Limitations statement
- Unresolved questions
- Alternative explanations
- Future investigation proposal

These activity patterns are candidates, not mandatory modules. The final course should select a coherent subset according to its primary purpose, intended learner level, and available instructional time.

## 7. Candidate Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

### 7.1 Candidate Inclusion Criteria

A topic, method, tool, or artifact should be considered for inclusion when it:

1. Supports a clearly identified learning outcome.
2. Strengthens inquiry, critical interpretation, evidence-based reasoning, discussion, or communication.
3. Helps students move beyond unsupported opinion or superficial summary.
4. Produces observable evidence of learning.
5. Is appropriate to the intended learner profile and prerequisite level.
6. Can be completed meaningfully within the available time.
7. Is feasible under available staffing, resources, and ethical constraints.
8. Contributes to Seminar's distinctive role within the curriculum.

### 7.2 Candidate Exclusion or Deferral Criteria

A topic should be excluded or deferred when it:

1. Adds theoretical complexity without improving the intended learning outcome.
2. Requires research methods beyond the learners' preparation or available supervision.
3. Duplicates another course without a clear inquiry or communication purpose.
4. Requires unavailable databases, equipment, participants, or specialist support.
5. Introduces unnecessary privacy, safety, consent, or integrity risks.
6. Rewards source quantity or presentation polish over reasoning quality.
7. Requires publication-level novelty or rigor without an appropriate instructional basis.
8. Cannot be assessed reliably using available evidence.

### 7.3 Minimum Viable Inquiry Loop

A viable seminar learning cycle should normally preserve the following elements:

- A meaningful question, problem, or claim
- Relevant sources or evidence
- Some form of critical interpretation or reasoning
- A communicated conclusion, position, or finding
- Acknowledgment of relevant limitations or uncertainty

Where discussion is the primary purpose, the cycle may emphasize preparation, evidence-based exchange, and reflection.

Where research is the primary purpose, it may include a more explicit method, evidence collection or analysis, and written or oral research output.

A formal paper, original experiment, or novel research contribution is not mandatory in every seminar format.

## 8. Open Questions for Subsequent Modeling

The following questions remain unresolved and should not be prematurely fixed at the inventory stage.

### A. Course Identity

- Is Seminar primarily discussion-oriented, research-oriented, project-reflection-oriented, or presentation-oriented?
- Should one mode define the core course while others function as supporting activities?
- Is Seminar intended as a standalone core course, a recurring learning format, or a course that integrates with project-based learning?
- What is its distinctive contribution beyond the research and communication activities already present in other courses?

### B. Learner Profile

- What age, academic preparation, and reading ability should be assumed?
- What level of English-language academic reading and writing is realistic?
- Should students work with instructor-selected sources, self-selected sources, or a combination?
- How much scaffolding is required for research-question development and argument construction?

### C. Research Depth

- Is a structured literature review sufficient, or should students conduct a small-scale investigation?
- Must students produce an original research question?
- Is a formal research proposal required?
- Which research methods can be taught and supervised reliably?
- What distinguishes a successful introductory inquiry from a more advanced research project?
