## 1. Domain Scope and Positioning

### 1.1 Working Definition

Industrial Design is a human-centered, materially grounded, and interdisciplinary design discipline concerned with conceiving, developing, evaluating, and communicating products and product-related systems.

It integrates user needs, function, form, ergonomics, interaction, materials, manufacturing constraints, aesthetics, cultural meaning, commercial considerations, and environmental responsibility.

Industrial Design is not limited to styling the external appearance of an object. It addresses how a product works, how people perceive and use it, how it can be produced, and what value it creates for users, manufacturers, and society.

### 1.2 Primary Scope

The candidate cognition space includes:

- Industrial design foundations and disciplinary history.
- Design research, problem framing, and opportunity identification.
- Human factors, ergonomics, and physical interaction.
- Product semantics, form development, and visual communication.
- Product architecture and conceptual development.
- Materials, manufacturing processes, and fabrication awareness.
- Digital modeling, rendering, and physical prototyping.
- Design evaluation, iteration, and validation.
- Product development, production feasibility, and interdisciplinary collaboration.
- Sustainability, lifecycle thinking, and responsible design.
- Smart products, connected devices, and emerging physical-digital experiences.
- Design communication, documentation, and portfolio development.

### 1.3 Boundary with Adjacent Domains

Industrial Design overlaps with several neighboring disciplines but should retain its own design-centered perspective.

| Adjacent Domain | Relationship | Boundary Principle |
|---|---|---|
| HCI / UX / UI | Human needs, interaction, usability, experience | Industrial Design emphasizes physical products, embodied use, and material form; digital interaction remains a major interface with HCI |
| Mechanical Engineering | Structures, mechanisms, tolerances, physical feasibility | Industrial Design considers engineering constraints but does not replace detailed engineering analysis |
| Robotics | Embodied systems, human-robot interaction, product form | Industrial Design addresses form, use, physical interfaces, and user-facing qualities; robotics owns specialized autonomy, control, and system integration |
| Modern Software Development | Connected products, embedded interfaces, companion applications | Software implementation and architecture remain separate areas of expertise |
| Applied AI and Intelligent Systems | Smart products, adaptive behavior, AI-enabled interaction | AI methods and model engineering belong primarily to the AI cognition space |
| Business Intelligence and Analytics | Market evidence, product performance, customer and business data | Industrial Design uses evidence to inform design decisions without absorbing the full analytics discipline |
| Foundation Core | General design reasoning, modeling, communication, engineering foundations | Shared foundations should be reused rather than duplicated unnecessarily |
| Advanced Core | Integrated system design, abstraction, verification, complex problem-solving | Advanced methods may support product-system projects without becoming exclusively Industrial Design topics |

### 1.4 Important Distinctions

- Industrial Design is not merely aesthetic styling.
- Product design is often used as a broader or overlapping term; its exact scope varies by institution and industry.
- Design thinking is a useful family of approaches, not a substitute for domain-specific design knowledge.
- CAD proficiency does not by itself demonstrate product-design competence.
- A visually convincing rendering does not establish ergonomic, functional, manufacturing, or safety feasibility.
- A prototype is an instrument for learning and evaluation, not automatically a production-ready product.
- Sustainable intent does not establish environmental performance without evidence.
- The inventory is a candidate map, not a claim that every listed topic is required in every curriculum.

## 5. D4 — Processes and Methods

### 5.1 Design Brief and Project Definition

- Define the project context and intended users.
- Identify stakeholders and use environments.
- Establish user needs and opportunity areas.
- Define the problem statement.
- Establish constraints and success criteria.
- Identify technical, commercial, and environmental considerations.
- Develop a design brief.
- Record assumptions, unknowns, and dependencies.

### 5.2 Research and Synthesis

- Plan the research.
- Collect primary and secondary evidence.
- Observe existing behavior and product use.
- Analyze competing products and alternatives.
- Identify recurring needs, failures, and opportunities.
- Synthesize findings into design requirements.
- Validate interpretations with relevant stakeholders.
- Distinguish observed evidence from assumptions.

### 5.3 Ideation and Concept Development

- Generate multiple concept directions.
- Explore form, function, interaction, and configuration.
- Use sketches and rough models to externalize ideas.
- Compare concepts against explicit criteria.
- Identify critical uncertainties.
- Select concepts for further investigation.
- Develop and communicate concept rationale.
- Revisit the problem framing when evidence requires it.

### 5.4 Form and Product Development

- Develop proportions, silhouette, and product identity.
- Explore physical interaction and ergonomics.
- Consider internal packaging and component constraints.
- Develop CAD models and spatial layouts.
- Define key interfaces and functional relationships.
- Refine CMF and surface details.
- Assess manufacturability and assembly implications.
- Maintain consistency between form, function, and use context.

### 5.5 Prototyping and Iteration

- Select prototype fidelity based on the question being tested.
- Build appearance, fit, or functional prototypes.
- Test with representative users or relevant technical conditions.
- Observe failures, friction, misuse, and unintended behavior.
- Record results and limitations.
- Revise requirements or design decisions.
- Repeat until the defined criteria are sufficiently supported.
- Avoid treating visual polish as proof of functional maturity.

### 5.6 Materials, Manufacturing, and Feasibility

- Identify candidate materials and processes.
- Compare performance, cost, appearance, and sustainability.
- Check critical dimensions and interfaces.
- Evaluate assembly and maintenance.
- Identify tooling and production constraints.
- Consult engineering and manufacturing specialists.
- Refine the design for production feasibility.
- Document unresolved technical risks.

### 5.7 Evaluation and Design Verification

- Define measurable or observable evaluation criteria.
- Verify requirements where appropriate.
- Evaluate usability, ergonomics, function, and appearance separately when necessary.
- Check physical fit and interface consistency.
- Evaluate safety and foreseeable misuse.
- Review manufacturing and maintenance implications.
- Compare the final design against the original brief.
- Record residual limitations and unverified assumptions.

### 5.8 Communication and Handoff

- Prepare concept narratives and design rationale.
- Produce sketches, renderings, models, and prototypes.
- Create annotated drawings and relevant specifications.
- Communicate material, finish, and assembly intent.
- Document requirements and known constraints.
- Prepare files and evidence for engineering or manufacturing handoff.
- Present trade-offs and unresolved questions transparently.
- Develop a portfolio case study showing process and outcomes.

## 8. D7 — Competencies and Performance

### 8.1 Research and Problem Definition

- Identify and articulate user needs.
- Investigate the context of product use.
- Distinguish symptoms from underlying problems.
- Convert research findings into design requirements.
- Define constraints and evaluation criteria.
- Recognize assumptions and evidence gaps.
- Reframe the problem when new evidence warrants it.

### 8.2 Design Exploration and Creativity

- Generate multiple viable concepts.
- Explore alternatives rather than committing prematurely.
- Translate abstract ideas into sketches and models.
- Integrate function, form, use, and context.
- Explain the rationale behind design decisions.
- Balance originality with usefulness and feasibility.

### 8.3 Human-Centered Product Design

- Apply basic anthropometric and ergonomic reasoning.
- Consider accessibility and human variability.
- Design understandable physical controls and feedback.
- Evaluate comfort, usability, and perceived quality.
- Anticipate foreseeable errors and misuse.
- Incorporate user evidence into design iteration.

### 8.4 Form and Visual Communication

- Develop coherent product form.
- Use proportion, scale, and visual hierarchy intentionally.
- Communicate form through sketches, drawings, and renderings.
- Select and justify CMF directions.
- Connect visual decisions with function and user context.
- Communicate design intent clearly to non-design stakeholders.

### 8.5 Technical and Material Literacy

- Select plausible materials and manufacturing processes.
- Recognize basic structural, spatial, and assembly constraints.
- Build appropriate digital models and prototypes.
- Understand the difference between appearance and functional validation.
- Identify when specialist engineering input is required.
- Consider production feasibility during early design.

### 8.6 Iteration and Evaluation

- Select prototypes appropriate to the question being investigated.
- Define and conduct basic evaluation activities.
- Observe and interpret test outcomes.
- Revise designs in response to evidence.
- Explain trade-offs and unresolved risks.
- Avoid unsupported claims of usability, safety, or feasibility.

### 8.7 Sustainability and Responsibility

- Consider lifecycle impacts.
- Identify opportunities for durability, repair, reuse, and recycling.
- Compare material and process alternatives.
- Recognize environmental and social trade-offs.
- Avoid treating sustainability as a purely visual or marketing attribute.
- Communicate limitations in sustainability evidence.

### 8.8 Collaboration and Professional Communication

- Collaborate with engineering, software, business, and research teams.
- Communicate requirements and constraints.
- Produce clear design documentation.
- Receive and provide constructive critique.
- Explain decisions to different audiences.
- Maintain a traceable relationship between the brief, design decisions, and final deliverables.

## 9. D8 — Levels and Prerequisites

The following levels are provisional modeling categories, not a standardized academic classification.

### Level A — Awareness and Foundations

Typical capabilities:

- Explain the role and scope of Industrial Design.
- Recognize basic design elements and principles.
- Identify common product materials and manufacturing processes.
- Observe basic user-product interactions.
- Produce simple sketches and visual references.
- Understand the purpose of a design brief and prototype.

Possible prerequisites:

- General observation and communication.
- Basic visual literacy.
- No advanced CAD or engineering knowledge required.

### Level B — Guided Application

Typical capabilities:

- Conduct basic user and context research.
- Translate a simple brief into requirements.
- Generate multiple product concepts.
- Apply introductory ergonomic reasoning.
- Produce basic CAD models or physical mock-ups.
- Compare concepts against explicit criteria.
- Communicate a design rationale.

Possible prerequisites:

- Level A foundations.
- Basic drawing, modeling, and documentation skills.

### Level C — Integrated Product Development

Typical capabilities:

- Integrate user research, form, function, and physical interaction.
- Develop a product concept through iterative modeling.
- Select plausible materials and manufacturing processes.
- Build and evaluate functional or ergonomic prototypes.
- Revise the design based on evidence.
- Communicate technical and experiential trade-offs.

Possible prerequisites:

- Level B application.
- Basic CAD and prototyping literacy.
- Introductory understanding of materials and manufacturing.

### Level D — Advanced and Cross-Disciplinary Design

Typical capabilities:

- Address complex or competing design requirements.
- Integrate product architecture, manufacturing, sustainability, and user experience.
- Coordinate with engineering and other disciplines.
- Evaluate uncertainty and design risk.
- Develop connected or technically complex product concepts.
- Produce a coherent evidence-based design-development package.

Possible prerequisites:

- Level C integrated development.
- Relevant technical foundations.
- More advanced research, evaluation, and collaboration skills.

### Level E — Specialization and Independent Practice

Possible specialization areas:

- Consumer electronics and smart products.
- Transportation and mobility products.
- Furniture and lifestyle products.
- Medical and assistive products.
- Wearables and body-integrated devices.
- Robotics and physical-digital products.
- Sustainable product systems.
- Advanced materials and manufacturing.
- Product-service systems and strategic design.

Typical capabilities:

- Define and manage a specialized design problem.
- Select appropriate research and development methods.
- Coordinate specialist contributions.
- Evaluate complex technical, human, commercial, and environmental trade-offs.
- Defend design decisions using relevant evidence.
- Produce a portfolio demonstrating process, outcomes, and limitations.

## 10. Cross-Domain Connections

### 10.1 Industrial Design ↔ HCI / UX / UI

Shared concerns:

- Human-centered design.
- User research.
- Usability and experience.
- Interaction and feedback.
- Accessibility and inclusive design.
- Prototyping and evaluation.

Industrial Design emphasis:

- Physical form and material qualities.
- Ergonomics and embodied interaction.
- Product appearance, handling, and physical controls.

HCI / UX / UI emphasis:

- Digital interaction, information structures, interface behavior, and broader human-computer relationships.

Boundary warning:

A physical product with a digital interface often requires both cognition spaces. Neither should be reduced to the other.

### 10.2 Industrial Design ↔ Modern Software Development

Shared concerns:

- Requirements and constraints.
- Iterative development.
- Prototyping.
- Testing and feedback.
- Product delivery and collaboration.

Connection areas:

- Connected consumer products.
- Companion applications.
- Digital product interfaces.
- Embedded software and device interaction.
- AI-assisted design and development workflows.

Boundary warning:

Industrial Design may specify user-facing behavior and physical interfaces without owning software architecture or implementation.

### 10.3 Industrial Design ↔ Applied AI and Intelligent Systems

Connection areas:

- Smart products.
- AI-enabled devices.
- Adaptive interfaces and behaviors.
- Intelligent assistants embodied in physical products.
- Generative design and AI-assisted visualization.
- AI-supported research and concept exploration.

Boundary warning:

Using AI tools during design is different from designing an AI-enabled product. The former is a workflow concern; the latter may require deeper understanding of AI capabilities, limitations, and system behavior.

### 10.4 Industrial Design ↔ Robotics

Shared concerns:

- Physical embodiment.
- Human-product or human-robot interaction.
- Form, handling, and physical accessibility.
- Safety and foreseeable misuse.
- Physical prototyping and evaluation.

Industrial Design emphasis:

- User-facing form, physical controls, approachability, handling, and experience.

Robotics emphasis:

- Kinematics, dynamics, sensing, actuation, control, autonomy, and robotic-system integration.

### 10.5 Industrial Design ↔ Mechanical Engineering

Shared concerns:

- Physical feasibility.
- Component arrangement.
- Mechanisms and interfaces.
- Materials and manufacturing.
- Tolerances, assembly, and reliability.

Industrial Design emphasis:

- Human needs, product form, physical experience, and design intent.

Mechanical Engineering emphasis:

- Detailed mechanical analysis, structural performance, engineering calculations, and technical verification.

Boundary warning:

Basic engineering literacy is essential for responsible product design, but specialist calculations and certification should not be implied by a general Industrial Design learning outcome.

### 10.6 Industrial Design ↔ Business Intelligence and Analytics

Connection areas:

- Market and competitor research.
- User and customer evidence.
- Product performance data.
- Product adoption and feedback.
- Product segmentation and positioning.
- Evidence-informed prioritization.

Boundary warning:

Industrial designers should understand and use relevant evidence without being expected to master the complete analytics discipline.

### 10.7 Industrial Design ↔ Foundation Core

Potential shared foundations:

- Creative and critical thinking.
- Visual communication.
- Research literacy.
- Basic mathematical and scientific reasoning.
- Systems thinking.
- Modeling and abstraction.
- Technical communication.
- Ethics and sustainability.

### 10.8 Industrial Design ↔ Advanced Core

Potential advanced integrations:

- Complex product-system design.

- Multidisciplinary design integration.

## 11. Coverage Audit and Selection Readiness

This section is intended to help inspect the inventory, not to certify its completeness.

### 11.1 Coverage Questions

Before using the inventory to construct a curriculum, examine whether it adequately represents:

- Disciplinary foundations and design history.
- Human needs, physical interaction, and ergonomics.
- Form development and visual communication.
- Product architecture and functional reasoning.
- Materials, manufacturing, and production constraints.
- Research, prototyping, testing, and iteration.
- Sustainability and lifecycle thinking.
- Product communication and interdisciplinary collaboration.
- Smart products and emerging physical-digital contexts.

### 11.2 Common Modeling Risks

**Risk 1 — Reducing Industrial Design to Appearance**

A strong visual presentation cannot substitute for user evidence, functional reasoning, ergonomics, or feasibility.

**Risk 2 — Absorbing Mechanical Engineering**

Industrial Design needs technical literacy and collaboration, but should not duplicate an entire mechanical engineering curriculum.

**Risk 3 — Treating Tools as Competencies**

Knowing a CAD package or rendering tool is not equivalent to demonstrating product-design judgment.

**Risk 4 — Confusing Prototype Fidelity with Evidence Quality**

A high-fidelity prototype can still test the wrong question or provide misleading evidence.

**Risk 5 — Treating Sustainability as a Label**

Sustainability requires lifecycle reasoning and transparent trade-offs, not merely the selection of a material marketed as environmentally friendly.

**Risk 6 — Overloading the Candidate Inventory**

The inventory should preserve potentially relevant knowledge, but not every item belongs in the eventual course package.

**Risk 7 — Ignoring Cross-Domain Ownership**

Smart products and connected devices may require coordinated contributions from Industrial Design, HCI, software, AI, robotics, and engineering.

**Risk 8 — Mixing Abstraction Levels**

Disciplinary fields, theories, methods, tools, artifacts, and competencies should remain distinguishable even when they are related.

### 11.3 Inventory Versus Curriculum

The inventory answers:

- What knowledge, methods, tools, artifacts, and competencies might matter in this domain?
- What relationships exist among these elements and neighboring domains?
- What candidate prerequisites and capability levels may be relevant?

The curriculum-construction process must separately answer:

- Which outcomes are appropriate for the target learners?
- Which topics are necessary to achieve those outcomes?
- Which topics can be omitted or deferred?
- What depth and duration are realistic?
- Which activities and deliverables demonstrate learning?
- What resources, instructors, facilities, and safety controls are required?
- How will achievement be evaluated?

### 11.4 Selection Readiness Checklist

- [ ] Domain scope and boundaries are sufficiently clear.
- [ ] Major knowledge and practice areas have been inventoried.
- [ ] The eight observation dimensions have been considered.
- [ ] Candidate omissions and duplications have been reviewed.
- [ ] Cross-domain dependencies have been identified.
- [ ] Candidate prerequisite chains are plausible.
- [ ] Tools are distinguished from transferable competencies.
- [ ] Artifacts are distinguished from learning outcomes.
- [ ] Safety and resource implications are visible.
- [ ] Curriculum-specific inclusion decisions remain open.
