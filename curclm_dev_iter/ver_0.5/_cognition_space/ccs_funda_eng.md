## 0. Purpose and Scope

### 0.1 Purpose

This document establishes an initial candidate cognition space for
**Fundamentals of Engineering**.

It inventories potentially relevant disciplinary knowledge, concepts,
theories, research methods, processes, tools, artifacts, competencies,
and prerequisite levels that may inform the subsequent development of
the course syllabus, learning activities, assessments, and course materials.

The inventory is intended to support:

- Course-boundary definition and curriculum modeling.
- Identification of foundational engineering knowledge and practices.
- Alignment between learning outcomes, activities, artifacts, and assessment.
- Differentiation between foundational and advanced learning expectations.
- Subsequent curriculum construction and AI-assisted content generation.

### 0.2 Working Course Definition

Fundamentals of Engineering introduces learners to the ways engineers
understand problems, apply scientific and mathematical knowledge,
evaluate constraints, develop solutions, and assess whether proposed
solutions work under real or simulated conditions.

The course should establish a transferable engineering mindset rather
than train learners exclusively in one engineering discipline.

Its central concern is the relationship among:

- Problems and needs.
- Scientific and mathematical principles.
- Engineering assumptions and constraints.
- Proposed solutions and implementations.
- Evidence, testing, and evaluation.
- Safety, responsibility, and real-world consequences.

### 0.3 Candidate Scope

The initial inventory considers the following knowledge areas:

1. Engineering as a discipline and professional practice.
2. Scientific reasoning and quantitative foundations.
3. Engineering problem formulation and problem solving.
4. Measurement, experimentation, and evidence.
5. Materials, energy, forces, and physical systems.
6. Information, computation, and technological systems.
7. Design iteration and engineering implementation.
8. Reliability, safety, sustainability, and responsible practice.
9. Communication, collaboration, and technical documentation.

These areas are candidate domains, not a commitment to include every
topic in the final course.

### 0.4 Scope Boundaries

This course establishes foundational understanding and practice.

It should not become a comprehensive substitute for:

- **Systems Design and Abstract Modeling** — formal system boundaries,
  abstraction, structural and behavioral models, system architecture,
  and systematic analysis of complex systems.
- **Applied Convergence of Design and Technology** — deeper integration
  of design processes, user needs, technologies, prototypes, and
  application-oriented outcomes.
- **Seminar** — focused investigation of engineering best practices,
  computational mathematics, and selected advanced principles.

These boundaries are provisional. Some overlap is necessary because
foundational engineering problems naturally involve systems, design,
computation, and professional practice.

# 1. Disciplinary Knowledge

This dimension identifies candidate bodies of knowledge that help
learners understand what engineering is and how engineering problems
are approached.

## 1.1 Engineering Foundations

- Definitions and purposes of engineering.
- Engineering as the application of scientific and mathematical knowledge.
- Relationships among science, engineering, technology, and design.
- Major engineering disciplines and interdisciplinary engineering.
- Engineering problem solving versus scientific investigation.
- Engineering artifacts, infrastructure, processes, and services.
- Engineering innovation and technological development.
- Historical development of engineering practices.
- Relationships between engineering solutions and societal needs.
- Engineering practice across laboratory, industrial, digital, and
  environmental contexts.

## 1.2 Scientific Foundations

- Physical quantities and units.
- Matter, materials, and basic material properties.
- Forces, motion, and elementary mechanics.
- Energy, work, power, and efficiency.
- Heat, temperature, and elementary thermodynamics.
- Electricity and basic electrical phenomena.
- Waves, signals, and elementary physical interactions.
- Chemical properties and material transformations, where relevant.
- Living systems and biological principles, where relevant to an
  engineering application.
- Environmental systems and physical resource constraints.

The final syllabus should select scientific principles according to
the intended engineering examples rather than attempt to teach an
entire introductory science curriculum.

## 1.3 Mathematical and Quantitative Foundations

- Arithmetic, algebra, and proportional reasoning.
- Ratios, rates, percentages, and dimensional consistency.
- Geometry and spatial reasoning.
- Functions, variables, and graphical relationships.
- Coordinate systems and vector concepts.
- Elementary trigonometry.
- Estimation and order-of-magnitude reasoning.
- Measurement uncertainty and significant figures.
- Basic descriptive statistics.
- Tables, charts, and quantitative interpretation.
- Introductory probability and risk reasoning.
- Elementary optimization and trade-off reasoning.

Calculus, linear algebra, differential equations, and numerical
methods may be relevant to selected applications, but their formal
instruction should depend on learner readiness and the course's
actual learning objectives.

## 1.4 Materials and Physical Components

- Material categories and material properties.
- Strength, stiffness, hardness, toughness, and durability.
- Weight, density, and dimensional properties.
- Material selection under functional constraints.
- Components, assemblies, and physical interfaces.
- Fasteners, joints, and elementary mechanisms.
- Manufacturing and fabrication considerations.
- Material availability, cost, reuse, and recyclability.
- Relationships between material choice and product performance.

## 1.5 Energy and Physical Resources

- Energy forms and transformations.
- Energy input, useful output, and losses.
- Power requirements and resource consumption.
- Elementary energy efficiency.
- Electrical and mechanical power in practical systems.
- Renewable and non-renewable energy concepts.
- Resource constraints and environmental implications.
- Energy-related safety considerations.

## 1.6 Information and Computation Foundations

- Information representation and interpretation.
- Data as observations, measurements, or records.
- Sensors, inputs, outputs, and elementary control.
- Logic, algorithms, and procedural reasoning.
- Software and hardware as interacting components.
- Computational tools for calculation and visualization.
- Digital representation of physical or abstract phenomena.
- Basic automation and feedback concepts.
- Data quality and limitations of computational results.

This section provides engineering literacy. It does not imply that the
course must become a full programming or computer science course.

## 1.7 Engineering Context and Constraints

- Functional requirements.
- Physical and environmental constraints.
- Time, budget, and resource limitations.
- Human needs and operational contexts.
- Manufacturability and maintainability.
- Reliability and service life.
- Safety and regulatory considerations.
- Sustainability and lifecycle implications.
- Accessibility and usability considerations.
- Ethical, social, and economic consequences.

# 2. Concepts and Theories

This dimension identifies concepts and explanatory frameworks that
learners may use to reason about engineering problems.

## 2.1 Engineering Thinking

- Problem-oriented reasoning.
- Evidence-based decision making.
- Quantitative reasoning.
- Approximation and estimation.
- Decomposition of difficult problems into manageable tasks.
- Identification of assumptions.
- Distinction between facts, assumptions, hypotheses, and opinions.
- Recognition of uncertainty and incomplete information.
- Trade-off analysis.
- Iterative refinement.
- Learning from failure and unexpected results.

## 2.2 Scientific Reasoning

- Observation and evidence.
- Hypothesis formation.
- Variables and controlled comparisons.
- Cause, effect, and correlation.
- Measurement and uncertainty.
- Model-based explanation.
- Empirical validation.
- Reproducibility and repeatability.
- Limits of experimental conclusions.

Scientific investigation and engineering problem solving overlap,
but they are not identical: engineering must also address requirements,
constraints, feasibility, and the consequences of implementation.

## 2.3 Physical and Quantitative Reasoning

- Conservation of energy and other relevant conserved quantities.
- Force balance and elementary equilibrium.
- Motion and change over time.
- Relationships among input, output, and efficiency.
- Proportionality and scaling.
- Dimensional analysis and unit consistency.
- Sensitivity to changing variables.
- Linear and nonlinear relationships.
- Accumulation, rates of change, and feedback, at an appropriate level.
- Interpretation of measurement uncertainty.

The selected principles should be introduced through meaningful
engineering situations rather than as disconnected formulas.

## 2.4 Engineering Design Principles

- Need identification.
- Functional requirements and performance criteria.
- Feasibility and practicality.
- Constraints and design trade-offs.
- Alternative solution generation.
- Evaluation against explicit criteria.
- Iteration and refinement.
- Verification against specifications.
- Validation against intended use.
- Robustness under changing conditions.
- Simplicity, maintainability, and resource awareness.

Detailed design methodologies and formal design representations belong
primarily to the related design and systems courses.

## 2.5 Models and Representations

- Physical models.
- Conceptual representations.
- Mathematical models.
- Graphical representations.
- Tables and quantitative models.
- Simulation as an approximation of reality.
- Assumptions and simplifications.
- Model validity and limitations.
- Differences between model predictions and observations.

The emphasis here is on understanding why engineers use models and
how to interpret their limitations. Formal abstraction and system
modeling should be developed more deeply in Systems Design and
Abstract Modeling.

## 2.6 Systems Awareness

- Components and their interactions.
- Inputs, processes, outputs, and outcomes.
- Interfaces and dependencies.
- Feedback and control.
- Environmental context.
- Unintended consequences.
- Local improvements versus overall performance.

This is introductory systems literacy, not a substitute for formal
systems design, architecture, or systems analysis.

## 2.7 Reliability, Risk, and Safety

- Failure and failure consequences.
- Reliability and availability as distinct concepts.
- Hazards and exposure.
- Probability and severity of harm.
- Risk reduction and preventive measures.
- Safety margins and conservative assumptions.
- Redundancy and fault tolerance as introductory concepts.
- Testing under normal and adverse conditions.
- Limits of testing and prediction.

Formal reliability engineering and quantitative risk analysis are
advanced topics unless a particular learning activity requires them.

## 2.8 Sustainability and Responsibility

- Resource efficiency.
- Environmental impact.
- Lifecycle thinking.
- Repairability and durability.
- Reuse and recycling.
- Responsible use of technology.
- Ethical implications of engineering decisions.
- Stakeholder needs and conflicting interests.
- Social consequences and equitable access.
- Professional responsibility and accountability.

# 3. Research Methods

This dimension covers methods through which learners can investigate
problems, gather evidence, and evaluate engineering claims.

## 3.1 Problem Investigation

- Context observation.
- Background research.
- Stakeholder and user inquiry at an introductory level.
- Identification of the current situation.
- Problem statement development.
- Distinguishing symptoms from possible causes.
- Collection of relevant constraints and evidence.
- Comparison of existing solutions.
- Identification of unanswered questions.

## 3.2 Measurement and Data Collection

- Selection of measurable variables.
- Use of appropriate units.
- Selection and basic use of measuring instruments.
- Direct and indirect measurement.
- Repeated measurements.
- Recording observations systematically.
- Identification of measurement errors.
- Recognition of precision and accuracy.
- Data cleaning and basic organization.
- Documentation of experimental conditions.

## 3.3 Experimental Investigation

- Formulation of testable questions.
- Hypothesis development.
- Identification of independent, dependent, and controlled variables.
- Design of simple controlled experiments.
- Selection of test conditions.
- Replication and repeated trials.
- Comparison between predicted and observed outcomes.
- Interpretation of unexpected results.
- Recognition of experimental limitations.
- Safe conduct of practical investigations.

## 3.4 Quantitative Analysis

- Calculation and estimation.
- Unit conversion.
- Tabulation and visualization.
- Descriptive statistics.
- Comparison of alternatives using measurable criteria.
- Identification of trends and outliers.
- Basic error and uncertainty interpretation.
- Sensitivity checks.
- Interpretation of performance metrics.
- Explanation of conclusions using evidence.

## 3.5 Prototyping and Evaluation

- Construction of a proof of concept.
- Selection of an appropriate prototype fidelity.
- Testing a specific hypothesis or requirement.
- Observation of prototype behavior.
- Recording defects and unexpected outcomes.
- Comparison against acceptance criteria.
- Iterative modification.
- Documentation of changes.
- Evaluation of whether the prototype supports the intended claim.

## 3.6 Technical Investigation and Evidence Quality

- Identification of credible technical sources.
- Distinction between primary observations and secondary information.
- Basic source evaluation.
- Accurate recording of assumptions and procedures.
- Reproducible calculation and testing.
- Recognition of biased or incomplete evidence.
- Distinction between demonstrated findings and speculation.
- Transparent reporting of uncertainty and limitations.

# 4. Processes and Methods

This dimension identifies candidate workflows for carrying out
foundational engineering work.

## 4.1 General Engineering Problem-Solving Cycle

A candidate working cycle is:

1. Identify a need or problem.
2. Investigate context and gather evidence.
3. Define the problem and success criteria.
4. Identify constraints and assumptions.
5. Generate possible solutions.
6. Compare alternatives.
7. Select a feasible approach.
8. Build, calculate, simulate, or otherwise implement the solution.
9. Test and evaluate the result.
10. Refine the solution.
11. Communicate the outcome and limitations.

This is a flexible reasoning cycle, not a rigid waterfall process.
Some stages may be repeated, combined, or revisited as evidence changes.

## 4.2 Requirements and Constraints

- Translate a broad need into a specific problem statement.
- Distinguish required functions from preferred features.
- Identify measurable and qualitative criteria.
- Identify constraints and dependencies.
- Define assumptions explicitly.
- Establish preliminary acceptance criteria.
- Check whether requirements conflict.
- Revise requirements when evidence justifies revision.

Detailed requirements engineering and formal traceability may be
developed in later courses.

## 4.3 Solution Generation and Selection

- Brainstorming and structured ideation.
- Use of analogies and existing solutions.
- Generation of multiple alternatives.
- Preliminary feasibility assessment.
- Comparison using explicit criteria.
- Basic decision matrices.
- Cost, performance, risk, and time trade-offs.
- Selection and justification of a candidate solution.
- Recognition that no solution may satisfy every criterion.

## 4.4 Implementation and Iteration

- Plan tasks and required resources.
- Select suitable materials, components, or computational tools.
- Implement a small-scale solution.
- Maintain safe and organized working conditions.
- Record important design or implementation decisions.
- Test incremental changes where appropriate.
- Diagnose observable failures.
- Revise the implementation based on evidence.
- Preserve a usable record of the final state.

## 4.5 Testing and Evaluation

- Translate success criteria into observable tests.
- Select appropriate test methods.
- Establish test conditions.
- Record expected and actual results.
- Compare results against requirements.
- Identify defects, deviations, and limitations.
- Distinguish successful demonstration from comprehensive validation.
- Decide whether to accept, revise, or reject a solution.

## 4.6 Resource and Project Management

- Task decomposition at a basic level.
- Time estimation and scheduling.
- Resource and material planning.
- Cost estimation.
- Team role allocation.
- Progress tracking.
- Identification of blockers and risks.
- Scope adjustment under constraints.
- Completion criteria and handoff.

## 4.7 Documentation and Communication Workflow

- Record the initial problem and assumptions.
- Document calculations and methods.
- Maintain experiment and test records.
- Present evidence with appropriate figures and tables.
- Explain solution choices.
- State limitations and unresolved issues.
- Communicate findings to technical and non-technical audiences.
- Identify next steps based on the evidence.

- Scales and balances.
- Thermometers and temperature sensors.
- Timers and stopwatches.
- Multimeters and basic electrical measurement tools.
- Force or pressure sensors, where appropriate.
- Data loggers and digital sensors.

## 5.2 Mathematical and Data Tools

- Scientific calculators.
- Spreadsheets.
- Plotting and graphing software.
- Basic statistical tools.
- Unit conversion and dimensional calculation tools.
- Numerical calculation environments.
- Data acquisition and visualization software.

## 5.3 Physical Construction Tools

- Hand tools and basic workshop equipment.
- Measuring and marking tools.
- Cutting, joining, and fastening tools.
- Basic fabrication equipment.
- 3D printers and laser cutters, where available and appropriate.
- Materials for physical prototypes.
- Personal protective equipment.
- Equipment-specific safety procedures.

## 5.4 Digital Engineering Tools

- Basic drawing and diagramming software.
- Introductory CAD tools.
- Simulation and visualization software.
- Basic programming environments.
- Microcontrollers and sensors, where appropriate.
- Simple data acquisition platforms.
- Digital documentation and collaboration tools.

## 5.5 Research and Communication Tools

- Technical reference materials.
- Digital libraries and credible documentation sources.
- Laboratory notebooks or electronic records.
- Presentation and report-authoring software.

responsibility for submitted work.

# 7. Competencies and Performance

This dimension identifies what learners may be expected to understand,
do, explain, and evaluate.

## 7.1 Foundational Understanding

Learners may be expected to:

- Explain the role of engineering in addressing practical problems.
- Distinguish scientific investigation from engineering implementation.
- Recognize the relevance of mathematics and scientific principles.
- Identify common engineering disciplines and interdisciplinary contexts.
- Explain why engineering solutions must account for constraints.
- Recognize the limitations of assumptions, models, and measurements.

## 7.2 Problem Formulation

Learners may be expected to:

- Convert a broad need into a clear problem statement.
- Identify relevant stakeholders and operating conditions.
- Separate known information from assumptions and unknowns.
- Identify functional requirements and constraints.
- Establish preliminary success criteria.
- Explain why a proposed problem framing is appropriate.

## 7.3 Quantitative and Analytical Reasoning

Learners may be expected to:

- Select suitable equations or quantitative relationships.
- Use units consistently.
- Estimate plausible values before or alongside calculation.
- Interpret tables, graphs, and simple statistical summaries.
- Explain the assumptions behind a calculation.
- Compare analytical results with observations.
- Identify uncertainty and avoid unsupported precision.

## 7.4 Investigation and Experimentation

Learners may be expected to:

- Formulate a testable question.
- Design and conduct a basic investigation safely.
- Record measurements and observations systematically.
- Identify important sources of error.
- Interpret results using evidence.
- Explain the limitations of an experiment.
- Distinguish an observed result from a causal conclusion.

## 7.5 Solution Development

Learners may be expected to:

- Generate more than one possible solution.
- Compare alternatives against explicit criteria.
- Select a feasible approach and justify the choice.
- Implement a basic prototype or proof of concept.
- Test the implementation against defined criteria.
- Use feedback and test results to improve a solution.
- Explain why a solution remains incomplete or uncertain.

## 7.6 Engineering Judgment

Learners may be expected to:

- Recognize trade-offs among performance, cost, time, and resources.
- Identify foreseeable hazards and basic risk-reduction measures.
- Consider durability, maintainability, and environmental impact.
- Recognize the limits of personal expertise and available evidence.
- Avoid claiming that a solution is proven beyond the available tests.
- Explain the consequences of important engineering decisions.

## 7.7 Communication and Collaboration

Learners may be expected to:

- Communicate technical information clearly.
- Present calculations, diagrams, and evidence appropriately.
- Explain decisions to a non-specialist audience.
- Maintain understandable technical records.
- Contribute to collaborative problem solving.
- Respond constructively to critique and unexpected results.
- Identify individual contributions and shared responsibilities.

## 7.8 Reflective Practice

Learners may be expected to:

- Explain what was learned from a failed or incomplete attempt.
- Distinguish implementation problems from incorrect assumptions.
- Identify gaps in knowledge.
- Propose a reasonable next investigation or iteration.
- Reflect on the relationship between evidence and confidence.
- Recognize when additional expertise or more rigorous methods are needed.

# 8. Levels and Prerequisites

This dimension identifies possible entry requirements and progression
levels. It does not define the final instructional sequence.

## 8.1 Entry-Level Assumptions

Potential prerequisites or readiness indicators:

- Basic arithmetic and algebra.
- Ability to interpret simple graphs and tables.
- Basic familiarity with common physical phenomena.
- Ability to follow written and visual instructions.
- Basic digital literacy.
- Willingness to observe, test, document, and revise.
- Ability to work within basic laboratory or workshop safety rules.

Formal physics, advanced mathematics, or programming should not be
assumed universally unless required by a specific course variant.

## 8.2 Foundational Level

Possible learning expectations:

- Recognize common engineering problems and contexts.
- Apply basic quantitative and scientific reasoning.
- Identify requirements, assumptions, and constraints.
- Follow a structured investigation or problem-solving process.
- Construct and evaluate a simple proof of concept.
- Record observations and communicate conclusions.

## 8.3 Developing Level

Possible learning expectations:

- Independently formulate a bounded engineering problem.
- Compare multiple solutions using explicit criteria.
- Select appropriate measurement or analysis methods.
- Integrate evidence from calculation and experimentation.
- Improve a prototype through iterative testing.
- Explain trade-offs, uncertainties, and limitations.
- Coordinate a small engineering task or team activity.

## 8.4 Extension Level

Possible enrichment for learners with stronger preparation:

- Investigate more complex or interdisciplinary problems.
- Use elementary simulation or computational analysis.
- Compare analytical estimates with numerical or experimental results.
- Evaluate sensitivity to assumptions or parameter changes.
- Develop more explicit test and validation criteria.
- Investigate reliability, lifecycle, or sustainability considerations.
- Produce a more rigorous technical report.

Extension activities should deepen reasoning rather than simply increase
the amount of work or introduce advanced tools without purpose.

## 8.5 Progression Considerations

The course may support progression toward:

- Systems Design and Abstract Modeling.
- Applied Convergence of Design and Technology.
- Specialized engineering, robotics, computing, or design pathways.
- The Seminar course, where appropriate mathematical and analytical
  foundations have been established.

The exact progression should be determined by the final Core A and
Core B architecture, learner readiness, and course outcomes.

# 9. Cross-Dimensional Relationships

The eight dimensions are observational perspectives, not mutually
exclusive categories. A single engineering activity may involve
several dimensions simultaneously.

Examples:

| Engineering Activity | Relevant Dimensions |
|---|---|
| Investigating why a small bridge prototype bends | Disciplinary Knowledge; Concepts and Theories; Research Methods |
| Estimating the load a structure can support | Disciplinary Knowledge; Quantitative Reasoning; Tools and Technologies |
| Comparing two material choices | Concepts and Theories; Processes and Methods; Competencies |
| Building and testing a sensor-based device | Tools and Technologies; Research Methods; Artifacts and Deliverables |
| Revising a prototype after failure | Processes and Methods; Concepts and Theories; Competencies |
| Evaluating energy efficiency | Disciplinary Knowledge; Quantitative Analysis; Sustainability |
| Presenting test results and limitations | Artifacts and Deliverables; Competencies and Performance |

These relationships are intended to support later curriculum modeling.
They are not yet a formal dependency graph or a prescribed teaching order.

# 10. Candidate Learning-Activity Patterns

The following patterns may be used to explore the candidate cognition
space during later syllabus development.

They are illustrative activity patterns, not finalized course modules.

## 10.1 Measure, Estimate, and Explain

Learners measure a physical quantity, estimate its plausible range,
compare the two, and explain discrepancies.

Potential coverage:

- Measurement and units.
- Estimation and quantitative reasoning.
- Measurement uncertainty.
- Technical recording and explanation.

## 10.2 Build, Test, and Improve

Learners construct a simple artifact, define a measurable performance
criterion, test the artifact, and revise it.

Potential coverage:

- Requirements and constraints.
- Material or component selection.
- Prototyping and iteration.
- Testing and evaluation.
- Evidence-based decision making.

## 10.3 Compare Engineering Alternatives

Learners compare several candidate solutions under constraints such as
cost, performance, time, material use, and safety.

Potential coverage:

- Requirements and success criteria.
- Quantitative analysis.
- Trade-off reasoning.
- Decision matrices.
- Justification and communication.

## 10.4 Investigate a Failure

Learners examine a failed or underperforming artifact and distinguish
possible causes, unsupported assumptions, and missing evidence.

Potential coverage:

- Observation and hypothesis formation.
- Failure analysis at an introductory level.
- Experimental testing.
- Uncertainty and limitations.
- Iterative improvement.

## 10.5 Evaluate an Engineering Solution in Context

Learners examine a practical solution and consider whether it meets
its intended purpose under real constraints.

Potential coverage:

- Functional requirements.
- Stakeholder and environmental context.
- Reliability and safety.
- Sustainability and lifecycle considerations.
- Evidence-based evaluation.

The final selection should favor activities that integrate multiple
learning objectives without requiring an excessive number of unrelated
tools, artifacts, or technical topics.

# 11. Candidate Inclusion and Exclusion Rules

## 11.1 Inclusion Criteria

A candidate topic should receive stronger consideration when it:

- Supports a clearly stated course learning outcome.
- Establishes a reusable engineering concept or practice.
- Helps learners connect scientific or mathematical principles to
  practical problems.
- Can be explored through an appropriate activity or artifact.
- Is suitable for the intended learner population and available resources.
- Provides a meaningful foundation for subsequent courses.
- Can be assessed through observable evidence of learning.

## 11.2 Deferral or Exclusion Criteria

A topic may be deferred or excluded when it:

- Requires advanced prerequisites unavailable to the target learners.
- Is specific to a narrow engineering specialization without a clear
  foundational purpose.
- Duplicates another course's intended depth and primary responsibility.
- Requires tools, facilities, or supervision that are not available.
- Adds technical complexity without improving the learning outcome.
- Cannot be meaningfully assessed within the available course scope.
- Is included only because it appears in a conventional engineering
  textbook or curriculum.

A topic's presence in this inventory does not establish its priority.

# 12. Open Questions for Subsequent Curriculum Modeling

The following questions remain unresolved and should be addressed
through curriculum construction rather than assumed at the inventory stage.

1. What learner age, prior knowledge, and mathematical readiness should
   the course assume?

2. Should the course be primarily a broad engineering literacy course,
   a project-based introduction to engineering practice, or a deliberate
   combination of both?

3. Which scientific and mathematical principles are essential across
   all intended pathways?

4. How much physical prototyping, digital implementation, computation,
   and experimentation can the available teaching environment support?

5. What should distinguish the Foundation Core version from the
   Advanced Core version, if both use this course?

6. Which learning outcomes should be shared across both Core versions,
   and which should be extended or assessed at a higher level?

7. What is the smallest coherent set of learning activities and artifacts
   that can demonstrate the course's essential outcomes?

8. Which topics should be explicitly delegated to Systems Design and
   Abstract Modeling, Applied Convergence of Design and Technology,
   or Seminar?

9. What evidence should be required before a learner's engineering
   solution is considered adequately tested?

10. How should safety, sustainability, responsible technology use,
    and engineering ethics appear in the course's assessed work?

11. Which parts of the course should remain discipline-neutral, and
    which examples should be selected to connect with the concentrations?

12. What level of computational or AI-assisted work is appropriate
    without making tool proficiency a substitute for engineering
    understanding?

# 13. Current Status and Next Step

This document maps potential knowledge and practice areas for
Fundamentals of Engineering. It does not establish final topic priority,
course duration, weekly sequence, assessment weights, or Core-specific
variants.

The next modeling stage should:

1. Compare this inventory against the intended learner profile and
   curriculum requirements.
2. Select a manageable set of foundational learning outcomes.
3. Identify the smallest coherent set of learning activities and
   assessment artifacts.
4. Check boundaries and overlaps with the other three Core courses.
5. Define any differences between Foundation Core and Advanced Core.
6. Record unresolved decisions rather than silently assuming answers.

The objective is not to teach everything associated with engineering.
It is to establish a coherent, assessable, and extensible foundation
for subsequent engineering learning.
