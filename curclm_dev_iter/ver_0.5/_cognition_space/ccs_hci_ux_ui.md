# CCS: Human-Computer Interaction, User Experience, and Interface Design

## 1. Domain Scope and Positioning

This document defines the candidate cognition space for the Human-Computer Interaction, User Experience, and Interface Design concentration within the RXI curriculum system.

The scope extends from foundational undergraduate knowledge to advanced undergraduate practice, graduate-level theories and research methodologies, specialized technical practices, and emerging research areas. It provides a structured reference for identifying, classifying, and relating candidate knowledge entities before curriculum selection and development.

This document is a cognition-space inventory, not a finalized syllabus, course sequence, or prescribed set of learning outcomes. Inclusion in this inventory does not imply that a topic must be taught.

The domain encompasses three related but distinct areas:

- **Human-Computer Interaction (HCI):** The interdisciplinary study, design, implementation, and evaluation of interactions between people and computational systems. It includes human cognition, behavior, interaction techniques, computational systems, social contexts, and the consequences of technology use.
- **User Experience (UX):** The study and design of people's experiences with products, systems, services, and technologies, including usability, accessibility, expectations, emotions, trust, and experiences across tasks and touchpoints.
- **User Interface (UI) Design:** The design and specification of the perceptible and interactive surfaces through which people access and control digital systems, including visual hierarchy, layout, typography, interaction states, information presentation, and interface behavior.

These areas overlap but are not interchangeable. HCI provides a broad interdisciplinary research and design domain; UX addresses experiences across interactions and contexts; UI design focuses more directly on interface representation and behavior.

Relevant neighboring disciplines include cognitive psychology, human factors, visual communication, information science, software engineering, statistics, social computing, and human-AI interaction.

### 9.6 Cross-Level Prerequisite Dependencies

Potential dependency chains to investigate include:

- Human perception and cognition → cognitive models of interaction → advanced cognitive evaluation
- Visual design principles → interface design → design-system architecture and governance
- User research foundations → research design → advanced experimental and mixed-methods research
- Basic statistics → inferential statistics → advanced experimental analysis
- Interaction design → prototyping → implementation and technical evaluation
- Information organization → information architecture → complex information-seeking and sensemaking systems
- Accessibility fundamentals → inclusive design practice → advanced accessibility evaluation
- HCI foundations → human-AI interaction → empirical research on trust, reliance, and collaboration
- Social interaction foundations → CSCW and social computing → advanced collaborative systems research
- Design iteration → Research through Design → research-artifact development and evaluation

These dependencies should be validated rather than assumed to be universally linear. Different research questions and project types may require different prerequisite paths.

## 11. Coverage Audit and Selection Readiness

Before this cognition space is used to construct courses, the inventory should be reviewed against the following criteria:

- **Coverage:** Are the major disciplinary foundations, methods, practices, and research areas represented?
- **Boundary clarity:** Are HCI, UX, UI, human factors, visual design, information architecture, and adjacent fields distinguished without artificially isolating them?
- **Level consistency:** Are foundational concepts, applied methods, advanced practices, and research-frontier topics distinguishable?
- **Prerequisite traceability:** Are important dependencies identified and open questions recorded?
- **Entity consistency:** Are theories, methods, tools, artifacts, competencies, and disciplines classified according to their semantic roles?
- **Overlap management:** Are shared concepts represented consistently and cross-referenced across domains?
- **Research coverage:** Are both established methods and specialized or emerging research approaches represented?
- **Implementation relevance:** Are design, prototyping, technical implementation, evaluation, and maintenance all considered?
- **Ethical completeness:** Are accessibility, inclusion, privacy, consent, fairness, and potential harms addressed?
- **Selection traceability:** Can subsequent curriculum decisions explain why a topic is included, excluded, simplified, or deferred?

The inventory is a living cognition-space reference. Its entries are candidate entities, not commitments to teach. The subsequent curriculum-development process will select and sequence content according to learner profiles, intended outcomes, prerequisite constraints, available time and resources, and institutional delivery capabilities.
