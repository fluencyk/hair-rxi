# Curriculum Cognition Space

## 1. Purpose and Scope

The Curriculum Cognition Space defines the observable knowledge space underlying the RXI curriculum system. It provides a structured reference for identifying, classifying, relating, and evaluating candidate knowledge, concepts, methods, technologies, engineering practices, artifacts, and competencies across the curriculum's core models and concentration areas.

It serves as a cognitive baseline for curriculum design and development, rather than a finalized syllabus or a prescribed list of learning outcomes. Its purpose is to establish a sufficiently broad and coherent view of the relevant knowledge space before curriculum content is selected, sequenced, and adapted to the target learners and delivery constraints.

## 2. Overall Structure: One General Space, Eight Observation Dimensions

The Cognition Space covers the following curriculum components:

- **Core-A:** The foundational core curriculum model.
- **Core-B:** The advanced core curriculum model.
- **Six Concentrations:** Human-Computer Interaction, User Experience, and Interface Design; Modern Software Development; Applied AI and Intelligent Systems; Robotics; Industrial Design and Engineering; and Business Intelligence and Analytics (BIA).

These components are investigated through eight complementary observation dimensions:

1. Disciplinary Knowledge
2. Concepts and Theories
3. Research Methods
4. Processes and Methods
5. Tools and Technologies
6. Artifacts and Deliverables
7. Competencies and Performance
8. Levels and Prerequisites

The eight dimensions are complementary perspectives on the same cognition space, not mutually exclusive categories. A knowledge entity may be relevant to multiple dimensions, curriculum components, or concentrations.

## 3. Boundary and Selection Principles

The Cognition Space is intentionally broader than the final curriculum. Identifying a knowledge entity does not imply that it must be taught. Candidate content will be progressively evaluated against learner profiles, intended learning outcomes, prerequisite dependencies, available time and resources, and institutional delivery capabilities.

Core-A and Core-B establish the foundational and advanced dimensions of the shared curriculum structure. Concentrations extend this structure into specialized disciplinary and interdisciplinary areas. Relationships and overlaps between these components should be recorded explicitly rather than eliminated through artificial boundaries.

The Cognition Space should support traceable curriculum decisions: what is included or excluded, why it is included or excluded, what prerequisites it requires, and how it relates to other knowledge entities. Its coverage and boundaries may evolve as further investigation and implementation reveal omissions, ambiguities, or changes in requirements.
