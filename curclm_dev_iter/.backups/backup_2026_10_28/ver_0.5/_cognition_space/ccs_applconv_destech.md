## 1. Purpose and Scope

This document establishes an initial candidate cognition-space inventory for **Applied Convergence of Design and Technology**.

Its purpose is to identify the potential knowledge domains, concepts, research methods, design processes, technologies, artifacts, and competencies that may contribute to the course.

This inventory is a discovery and modeling resource, not a finalized syllabus. Inclusion in this document does not imply that every topic must be taught, assessed, or represented in the final curriculum.

The inventory should support subsequent decisions about:

- Course identity and educational purpose
- Candidate learning outcomes
- Project-based learning structures
- Technology and design integration
- Student prerequisites and progression
- Assessment evidence and deliverables
- Feasible instructional and production requirements

The governing principle is to identify a sufficiently broad candidate space before narrowing it according to learner needs, course duration, available resources, and intended outcomes.

---

## 2. Working Course Definition

**Applied Convergence of Design and Technology** explores how design reasoning and technological capabilities can be integrated to address a real or realistically constructed need, opportunity, or problem.

Students investigate a context, frame a design challenge, develop possible solutions, select appropriate technologies, construct prototypes or other demonstrable outcomes, evaluate their performance, and communicate the rationale and evidence behind their decisions.

The course emphasizes the transition from **design intent to technological realization**, supported by iterative development and evidence-based evaluation.

Design and technology are treated as mutually informing dimensions:

- Design helps establish what should be created, for whom, why it matters, and what constitutes a meaningful experience or outcome.
- Technology provides capabilities, materials, computational processes, and implementation mechanisms through which a design can be realized.
- Integration requires students to negotiate the relationship between intended value, technical feasibility, usability, constraints, and evidence of performance.

The course may use digital, physical, computational, material, or hybrid project contexts. No single technology modality is assumed to be mandatory for every project.

## 3. Candidate Scope and Boundaries

### 3.1 Core Focus

The candidate scope includes:

1. Identifying needs, opportunities, and design challenges.
2. Investigating users, stakeholders, contexts, and existing solutions.
3. Translating design intentions into requirements and evaluation criteria.
4. Generating and comparing design alternatives.
5. Selecting appropriate technological capabilities and implementation approaches.
6. Integrating design decisions with technical decisions.
7. Developing prototypes, mock-ups, functional demonstrations, or other appropriate representations.
8. Testing and evaluating outcomes against defined criteria.
9. Iterating on the basis of observations, feedback, and technical evidence.
10. Communicating the design rationale, implementation, limitations, and results.

### 3.2 Relationship to Other Courses

| Related course | Primary emphasis | Distinction in this course |
|---|---|---|
| Fundamentals of Engineering | Engineering foundations, scientific reasoning, problem-solving, and introductory technical practice | Applies relevant engineering knowledge within a design-led, integrated project |
| Systems Design and Abstract Modeling | System boundaries, abstraction, requirements, system representations, architecture, and model evaluation | Uses appropriate system models when needed, but prioritizes the realization and evaluation of integrated outcomes |
| HCI / UX / UI | Human-computer interaction, user experience, interaction design, and interface quality | May use human-centered design, but does not require every project to be an interactive digital product |
| Modern Software Development | Software architecture, implementation, testing, and development practices | Software may serve as a design medium or implementation component without making software engineering the universal course focus |
| Applied AI and Intelligent Systems | AI capabilities, intelligent-system behavior, and application development | AI may be integrated where it serves the design objective, rather than being a mandatory component |
| Robotics | Physical computing, sensing, actuation, control, and robotic systems | Robotics may provide a project context without defining the whole course |
| Industrial Design and Technologies | Product form, materials, manufacturing, ergonomics, and product development | May draw on industrial design methods without becoming a comprehensive industrial design course |
| Business Intelligence and Analytics | Data acquisition, analysis, interpretation, and decision support | Data may inform design decisions or evaluation, but analytics is not necessarily the principal outcome |

These distinctions are provisional. The final boundaries should be checked against the complete curriculum architecture and the six concentration cognition-space inventories.

### 3.3 What This Course Is Not

The course should not automatically become:

- A survey of unrelated design disciplines and technologies.
- A software-development course with a design-themed project.
- A visual-design course in which technological feasibility is incidental.
- A fabrication course in which producing a physical object is treated as the only valid outcome.
- A specialist course in HCI, AI, robotics, or industrial design.
- A tool-training course organized primarily around software features.
- A project showcase without explicit requirements, evaluation criteria, or supporting evidence.

## 4.4 Processes and Methods

Candidate workflows for transforming a design challenge into a tested and communicated outcome.

### A. Problem-to-Opportunity Workflow

1. Identify a context, need, opportunity, or challenge.
2. Investigate relevant users, stakeholders, and existing conditions.
3. Define the problem and its boundaries.
4. Identify assumptions, constraints, and available resources.
5. Produce a design brief or equivalent problem statement.
6. Establish initial requirements and evaluation criteria.

### B. Concept Development Workflow

1. Generate multiple possible approaches.
2. Represent ideas through sketches, diagrams, storyboards, models, or equivalent means.
3. Compare alternatives against defined criteria.
4. Identify critical uncertainties and feasibility risks.
5. Select a candidate direction.
6. Document the rationale for the selection.

### C. Design-to-Technology Translation

1. Translate design intent into functional and experiential requirements.
2. Identify the technological capabilities needed.
3. Compare potential technologies, tools, materials, or platforms.
4. Evaluate feasibility, resource demands, and constraints.
5. Define an implementation strategy.
6. Identify integration dependencies and unresolved questions.

### D. Prototyping and Implementation

1. Select an appropriate prototype type and fidelity.
2. Build a low-cost representation or proof of concept.
3. Test the most important assumptions.
4. Implement or construct relevant components.
5. Integrate design and technical elements.
6. Record implementation decisions and difficulties.
7. Revise the prototype as evidence accumulates.

### E. Testing and Iteration

1. Define test questions and evaluation criteria.
2. Prepare suitable test conditions.
3. Observe or measure prototype behavior.
4. Compare results with intended outcomes.
5. Identify mismatches, failures, and improvement opportunities.
6. Prioritize revisions.
7. Repeat testing when useful and feasible.
8. Document remaining limitations.

### F. Outcome Communication

1. Explain the original problem and intended value.
2. Present the design concept and key decisions.
3. Demonstrate the outcome.
4. Explain how technology supports the design.
5. Present testing evidence and evaluation findings.
6. Discuss limitations, trade-offs, and unresolved issues.
7. Reflect on the development process and possible next steps.

**Modeling note:** The workflow is iterative rather than strictly linear. Findings from prototyping or testing may require students to revisit the problem framing, requirements, concept, or technology selection.

## 4.7 Competencies and Performance

Candidate capabilities students may develop and demonstrate.

### A. Problem Framing

- Identify a meaningful need or opportunity.
- Distinguish a surface-level request from an underlying problem.
- Identify relevant users, stakeholders, and contextual constraints.
- Formulate a clear and appropriately scoped design challenge.
- Translate a broad challenge into actionable requirements.

### B. Design Reasoning

- Generate and represent multiple solution concepts.
- Explain design decisions and intended user value.
- Compare alternatives using explicit criteria.
- Balance functional, experiential, aesthetic, and contextual considerations.
- Revise a design in response to new evidence.

### C. Technology Reasoning

- Identify technologies relevant to a design objective.
- Explain the capabilities and limitations of selected technologies.
- Assess technical feasibility at an appropriate level.
- Translate design intent into implementable requirements.
- Recognize dependencies, constraints, and integration risks.

### D. Integration and Development

- Coordinate design decisions with implementation decisions.
- Construct a prototype or demonstrable outcome.
- Select an appropriate level of fidelity.
- Integrate components or modalities where required.
- Diagnose and resolve implementation problems with suitable support.
- Make informed trade-offs among quality, time, cost, and complexity.

### E. Testing and Evaluation

- Establish meaningful evaluation criteria.
- Develop tests appropriate to the intended outcome.
- Collect and interpret relevant evidence.
- Identify mismatches between intention and actual behavior.
- Prioritize improvements based on evidence.
- Explain what has and has not been validated.

### F. Communication and Collaboration

- Communicate ideas through appropriate visual, verbal, and technical representations.
- Explain the relationship between design intent and technological implementation.
- Document decisions and development progress.
- Present and justify trade-offs.
- Receive and use critique constructively.
- Collaborate across design and technical tasks where applicable.

### G. Reflection and Responsibility

- Distinguish evidence from assumptions.
- Identify limitations and unresolved issues.
- Consider accessibility, safety, ethics, and sustainability where relevant.
- Reflect on how the design evolved.
- Identify meaningful next steps for further development.

**Assessment principle:** Competence should be evaluated through observable performance and supporting evidence, rather than inferred solely from a final presentation or artifact.

## 4.8 Levels and Prerequisites

The following progression is a candidate framework, not a finalized placement or grading system.

### Level 1 — Entry

Students can:

- Recognize basic design and technology examples.
- Describe a simple need or opportunity.
- Use supplied criteria to compare ideas.
- Follow guided instructions to create a simple representation or prototype.
- Explain the purpose of their outcome in basic terms.

Typical support: substantial instructor guidance, constrained tools, and clearly defined tasks.

### Level 2 — Foundational

Students can:

- Define a bounded design challenge.
- Identify basic user needs and technical constraints.
- Generate and compare multiple ideas.
- Select an appropriate technology from a guided set of options.
- Construct a basic prototype or demonstrable outcome.
- Conduct simple tests and record observations.
- Explain major design and implementation decisions.

Typical support: structured templates, guided decision-making, and instructor feedback at key milestones.

### Level 3 — Developing

Students can:

- Investigate a problem with greater independence.
- Translate design intent into requirements and evaluation criteria.
- Compare design and technology alternatives using explicit reasoning.
- Integrate multiple design or technical components where appropriate.
- Conduct iterative testing and use evidence to guide revisions.
- Document trade-offs and communicate limitations.
- Justify the final outcome against the original challenge.

Typical support: periodic guidance, with students owning the main project workflow.

### Level 4 — Extension

Students can:

- Address more ambiguous or interconnected challenges.
- Independently identify important uncertainties and risks.
- Evaluate competing approaches across multiple criteria.
- Integrate more complex technologies or implementation modalities.
- Design more systematic evaluation activities.
- Analyze unintended consequences and significant limitations.
- Propose credible next steps toward further development.

Typical support: greater student autonomy, with advanced methods introduced only when justified by the project.

### Candidate Prerequisites

Potential prerequisites include:

- Basic communication and visual representation skills
- Basic quantitative reasoning
- Introductory digital literacy
- Willingness to investigate, construct, test, and revise
- Ability to document work and explain decisions

Programming, CAD, fabrication, AI, electronics, or specialist design knowledge should not be assumed as universal prerequisites unless the eventual project specification requires them.

### Progression Principle

Progression should be defined primarily by:

- Problem complexity and ambiguity
- Independence of decision-making
- Depth of design-technology integration
- Technical and contextual constraints
- Quality of evaluation evidence
- Ability to explain trade-offs and limitations

It should not be defined simply by the number of tools used or the apparent sophistication of the final product.

## 5. Cross-Dimensional Relationships

The eight dimensions become educationally meaningful when connected through a coherent project workflow.

| Learning relationship | Relevant dimensions | Observable evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Understanding a need and its context | Disciplinary Knowledge; Research Methods | Research notes, context analysis, problem statement |
| Converting a need into a design challenge | Concepts and Theories; Processes and Methods | Design brief, requirements, assumptions, success criteria |
| Generating and comparing alternatives | Concepts and Theories; Research Methods; Competencies | Concept alternatives, comparison matrix, selection rationale |
| Connecting design intent to implementation | Disciplinary Knowledge; Processes and Methods; Tools and Technologies | Technology-selection rationale, technical requirements, implementation plan |
| Building a demonstrable outcome | Processes and Methods; Tools and Technologies | Prototype, implementation record, demonstration |
| Evaluating the outcome | Research Methods; Concepts and Theories; Competencies | Test plan, results, feedback, evaluation against criteria |
| Improving through iteration | Processes and Methods; Artifacts and Deliverables | Revision history, iteration log, before-and-after evidence |
| Communicating and reflecting | Artifacts and Deliverables; Competencies | Presentation, documentation, reflection, limitations analysis |

### Candidate End-to-End Learning Loop

**Context → Need → Design Challenge → Criteria → Alternatives → Technology Selection → Prototype → Test → Iterate → Communicate**

This loop is deliberately provisional. Depending on the project, students may revisit earlier stages, omit unnecessary stages, or use a different order.

The critical requirement is that design intent, technical realization, and evaluation remain connected rather than becoming separate activities.

## 6. Candidate Learning Activity Patterns

These patterns may help translate the cognition-space inventory into learning modules or project structures.

### Pattern A — Observe a Need, Define a Challenge

Students investigate a familiar environment, identify a meaningful need, and formulate a bounded design challenge.

Potential evidence:

- Context observations
- Need or opportunity statement
- Stakeholder considerations
- Initial design brief

### Pattern B — Design Intent to Technical Requirements

Students translate an intended experience, function, or outcome into explicit requirements and criteria.

Potential evidence:

- Design intent statement
- Functional and non-functional requirements
- Constraints and assumptions
- Evaluation criteria

### Pattern C — One Challenge, Multiple Solutions

Students generate several possible solutions and compare them against common criteria.

Potential evidence:

- Alternative concepts
- Concept representations
- Comparison matrix
- Selection rationale

### Pattern D — Technology as a Design Choice

Students investigate alternative technological approaches and explain why a particular approach is appropriate.

Potential evidence:

- Technology comparison
- Feasibility assessment
- Resource and risk considerations
- Implementation rationale

### Pattern E — Prototype to Learn

Students build a low-cost or low-fidelity prototype to investigate a critical assumption before investing in a more complete implementation.

Potential evidence:

- Prototype
- Test question
- Observation or measurement record
- Decision informed by the test

### Pattern F — Integrate Design and Technology

Students combine relevant design and technical components into a coherent demonstrable outcome.

Potential evidence:

- Integrated prototype or demonstration
- Component relationships
- Implementation notes
- Evidence of design-technology fit

### Pattern G — Test, Critique, and Iterate

Students evaluate an outcome, identify gaps, prioritize changes, and revise the design or implementation.

Potential evidence:

- Test results
- Feedback analysis
- Prioritized improvement list
- Revision history
- Explanation of remaining limitations

### Pattern H — Explain the Outcome and Its Trade-offs

Students present not only what they created but also why they created it, how technology enabled it, what evidence supports it, and what remains unresolved.

Potential evidence:

- Demonstration
- Design rationale
- Technical explanation
- Evaluation summary
- Limitations and future-development statement

These activity patterns are candidates, not mandatory modules. The final course should select a small number of coherent patterns that can be completed meaningfully within the available teaching time and resources.

## 7. Candidate Inclusion and Exclusion Criteria

### 7.1 Candidate Inclusion Criteria

A topic, method, tool, or artifact should be considered for inclusion when it:

1. Supports a clearly identified learning outcome.
2. Strengthens the integration of design reasoning and technological realization.
3. Helps students investigate, frame, develop, test, or communicate a meaningful solution.
4. Produces observable evidence of student learning.
5. Is appropriate to the intended learner profile and prerequisite level.
6. Can be taught and practiced within the available time.
7. Is feasible under available staffing, equipment, budget, licensing, and safety conditions.
8. Contributes meaningfully to the course's distinction from neighboring courses.

### 7.2 Candidate Exclusion or Deferral Criteria

A topic should be excluded or deferred when it:

1. Adds technical complexity without improving the intended learning outcome.
2. Requires specialist prerequisites that have not been established.
3. Duplicates another course without a clear applied purpose.
4. Depends on unavailable tools, facilities, or instructional expertise.
5. Introduces unnecessary safety, privacy, licensing, or operational risks.
6. Produces an artifact without meaningful investigation, implementation, or evaluation.
7. Is included mainly because a particular tool or technology is fashionable.
8. Cannot be assessed reliably within the available course structure.

### 7.3 Minimum Viable Learning Loop

A viable project should normally preserve the following minimum elements:

- A defined need or design challenge
- Explicit criteria or requirements
- At least one reasoned design or technology decision
- A demonstrable representation or outcome
- Some form of evaluation against the intended objective
- A documented conclusion, revision, or limitation

The exact form of each element may vary. A paper prototype, interactive mock-up, software demonstration, physical model, or integrated prototype may all be appropriate when aligned with the project objective.

## 8. Open Questions for Subsequent Modeling

The following questions remain unresolved and should not be prematurely fixed at the inventory stage.

### A. Learner Profile and Course Position

- What learner age, prior experience, and academic preparation should the course assume?
- Is the course intended for absolute beginners, students with some design or technical experience, or mixed cohorts?
- What should students be able to demonstrate before entering the course?
- What level of independence is realistic at the end?

### B. Course Identity

- What is the minimum distinctive capability that separates this course from Fundamentals of Engineering?
- How much formal systems modeling should be inherited from Systems Design and Abstract Modeling?
- Should the primary identity be interdisciplinary design practice, applied technology integration, or project-based engineering design?
- Which aspects of design should remain common across all projects, regardless of technology modality?

### C. Project and Technology Strategy

- Should students complete one substantial project or several smaller design-technology challenges?
- Should project contexts be student-selected, instructor-provided, or drawn from a common challenge bank?
- Which technology modalities are realistically supportable?
- Should the course offer a shared minimum technology pathway with optional extensions?
- What degree of implementation is required to count as a successful outcome?
- How should the course accommodate students with different technical starting points?

### D. Learning Outcomes and Assessment

- Which competencies are essential for every student?
- Which competencies are optional or project-dependent?
- What minimum evidence should be required for design reasoning, technical implementation, and evaluation?
- How should the course distinguish a visually polished outcome from a well-reasoned and well-tested outcome?
- How should individual contribution be assessed in collaborative projects?

### E. Delivery Constraints

- What are the available instructional hours?
- What is the realistic project budget?
- Which tools, materials, facilities, and software licenses are available?
- What safety and supervision requirements apply?
- How much instructor support is available during implementation and testing?
- Which deliverables can be reviewed and assessed consistently?

### F. Curriculum Architecture

- Does this course belong exclusively to an advanced or second-stage core, or can parts of it be adapted for introductory learners?
- How should its outcomes build upon the foundational engineering and systems-modeling courses?
- Which concentration-specific technologies should be available as project options without becoming mandatory course content?
- What knowledge and artifacts should be reusable by downstream concentration or portfolio courses?

## 9. Current Modeling Status

This document is a **v0.1 candidate cognition-space inventory**.

It establishes a provisional map of the knowledge, concepts, methods, processes, tools, artifacts, competencies, and progression levels that may contribute to Applied Convergence of Design and Technology.

It does not yet establish:

- A final course syllabus
- A fixed sequence of teaching modules
- Mandatory software or hardware platforms
- Final learning outcomes or assessment weights
- A fixed project specification
- A definitive Core A / Core B allocation

### Recommended Next Step

Complete the remaining candidate inventory, `ccs_seminar.md`, and then conduct a cross-course review of all four core-course cognition spaces.

The review should identify:

1. Shared foundations and dependencies.
2. Distinctive learning outcomes for each course.
3. Overlaps that are intentional versus overlaps that indicate duplication.
4. Knowledge and methods that should be prerequisites rather than repeated content.
5. Candidate course boundaries and progression between stages.
6. The smallest coherent set of outcomes and artifacts needed for each course.

Only after that review should the cognition spaces be narrowed into course-specific learning outcomes, module structures, and curriculum JSON.

**Design principle:** The purpose of this inventory is not to maximize the amount of content assigned to the course. It is to make the candidate space visible enough that subsequent scope decisions can be deliberate, explainable, and feasible.
